# Interpreting and reporting deviations

## What a map can support

Deviation maps are **descriptive placements** on a reference chart. In
research they support:

- individualised spatial summaries  
- linking extremes to symptoms, lesions, surgery, or outcome (with proper
  statistics)  
- comparing algorithms (concordance / discordance)

They do **not**, by themselves, establish diagnosis, prognosis, or surgical
targets (Rutherford et al., 2022; clinical caveats in applied papers).

## Quality checks emphasised in the protocol literature

1. Reference cohort documented (who is “normal”?)  
2. Covariates defined unambiguously  
3. Site adaptation reported when the site is new  
4. Control calibration after adaptation (mean/SD of Z)  
5. Thresholds pre-specified or clearly exploratory  
6. Multiplicity addressed for cohort tests on many regions/edges  

## Polarity and biology

Direction of Z depends on the phenotype. Thinner cortex (negative thickness
Z) and weaker connectivity (negative edge Z) are not universally “worse.”
Interpret in anatomical and clinical context (Marquand et al., 2019).

## Multi-model concordance

Because MFP, GAMLSS, and hierarchical Bayesian charts can disagree,
reporting cross-model agreement is good practice when multiple engines are
available (motivated by Ge et al., 2024; Rutherford et al., 2022).
